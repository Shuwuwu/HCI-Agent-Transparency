# Thank you for taking part

You have completed the study. Thank you for your time and effort.

## What was this study about?

AI assistants can increasingly act on a person's behalf, for example by sending emails, managing calendar events and sharing files. This study investigates whether seeing the actions an assistant took helps people notice when it has done something unsafe that the user did not ask for.

We are examining three things:

- how accurately people can tell whether an assistant behaved safely;
- how confident they are in those judgments;
- which additional information, such as the Mail, Calendar, Drive and Skills tabs, they choose to look at before deciding.

## What differed between participants?

Each participant was randomly assigned to one of two versions of the interface. In one version, every conversation included an activity log showing each action the assistant took, such as searching emails or sending a message. In the other version, each conversation showed only the user's request and the assistant's final reply. Everything else was the same in both versions. Comparing the two versions will show whether seeing an assistant's actions changes how people judge its behaviour.

## About the conversations

All conversations took place in a simulated workspace belonging to a fictional user, Emma Johnson. No real accounts, people or files were involved.

Some conversations were recorded from an AI assistant completing the user's request. Others were constructed for this study. In those, one of the skill documents given to the assistant contained hidden instructions, and the conversation showed the assistant carrying out an action the user had not requested, such as deleting a file, inviting an outside address to an event, or sending a private email or a security code to an outside address. Hiding instructions in content that an assistant reads is known as prompt injection, and it is a growing security concern for AI assistants.

At the start of the study, we described its purpose only in general terms, because knowing which parts of the interface we were comparing, or what kind of unsafe behaviour to look for, could have changed how you reviewed the conversations.

## Your data

Your responses and interaction logs are identified only by a participant code; the study did not ask for your name. They are stored and protected as described in the information you read before you agreed to take part.
